The **opposition** is more convincing.

Why: the anti-regulation side does a better job of showing that **strict regulation is too blunt a tool** for a fast-moving general-purpose technology like AI. Their strongest point is that harmful actors—criminals, hostile states, fraudsters—are unlikely to be stopped by compliance requirements, while the burden of strict rules would mainly fall on legitimate users, researchers, startups, hospitals, and schools. That is a clear practical objection: regulation may reduce benefits for responsible actors without meaningfully preventing misuse.

They also make a strong argument that AI evolves too quickly for heavy-handed regulation to remain effective. If rules are too rigid, they can become outdated fast, and in the meantime they may entrench large companies that can afford compliance, reducing competition and innovation. That is a serious downside that the pro side does not adequately answer.

The pro-regulation case is persuasive in highlighting real risks: misinformation, deepfakes, opacity, biased decisions, and profit incentives that can lead to unsafe deployment. But it relies more on the general need for oversight than on proving that **strict** regulation is the best response. The opposition effectively concedes that targeted oversight is appropriate, which weakens the motion’s absolute framing. If the choice is between broad strict regulation and more flexible, targeted governance, the opposition gives the better argument for the latter.

So, based purely on the arguments presented, **AI should not be strictly regulated**.
